## 1. The Chapter 3 concession — the one that touches a voice rule

**The situation.** The scoreboard table has a caption, and the caption's last
three sentences are you conceding something, in your own voice:

> *"One row Kai would add, and I concede it rather than let the table hide it:
> the regress's own rule — that the ladder-form holds at every depth — is not
> derived either… the reader is free to count it differently, and the table's
> verdict narrows if they do."*

In print this is unambiguous: a caption is the author talking, outside the
argument. In audio there are no captions. The words have to be spoken by
someone, and the table sits **inside an uninterrupted turn of Kai's**.

**Why I will not decide this.** The draft solves it by speaking the concession
under a **"Me."** label — which invents a turn in a dialogue that actually
happened. Your standing rule is that Chapter 3's concessions "actually happened
and must never be smoothed over"; manufacturing a turn is the mirror of
smoothing one away, and it is still a machine writing dialogue for you.

**The three options.**

- **(a) Keep the "Me." label** — simplest to listen to, and the words are
  genuinely yours. Cost: a listener will hear it as part of the argument, and
  it was not.
- **(b) Speak it as what it is — the author stepping outside the dialogue** —
  no speaker label, with a frame that says so. Draft: *"A word from outside the
  argument, in my own voice, because the table is mine and not Kai's: one row
  Kai would add…"* Cost: one extra sentence; breaks the spell briefly.
- **(c) Cut it.** Cost: unacceptable, I think — it is a concession, and
  concessions stay on the page. Listed only so the option is explicit.

**My recommendation: (b).** It is the only one that is both audible and true,
and the small break in the spell is the honest cost of having had the argument
for real.

## 3. Does the how-made note gain a sentence?

The note's rule is *"I would rather give it than have somebody find it out."*
The audio edition adds a new fact of construction: a machine is now also the
**voice**. The opening credits disclose it (item 4), which may be enough.

- **If yes**, it must be your sentence. A shape, to be rewritten or refused:
  *"If you are listening to this, add one more machine to the list: the voice."*
- **If no**, the audio edition is still honest — the disclosure is at its own
  front door.

**My recommendation: yes.** It costs one line, it sits in a paragraph already
listing what machines did, and it keeps the note true of every edition rather
than only the printed one. Against: it invites a sentence per format forever.

*Knock-on if yes:* one edit to the master tex → the ebook's v1.1 batch carries
it → the paperback interior changes at the next printing, not before.

## 4. The opening disclosure: short or long?

Full drafts in `CREDITS_DRAFT.md`. The choice is the third paragraph:

- **Long:** *"This edition is read by a synthetic voice. No human narrator was
  recorded, and the voice you are hearing is not the author's. The book has a
  note at the back about how it was made, and what a machine did and did not do
  in making it; this is the same disclosure, moved to the front, where you can
  act on it."*
- **Short:** *"This edition is read by a synthetic voice. No human narrator was
  recorded. The note at the back explains the rest, and why it is said here
  rather than discovered later."*

**My recommendation: short.** The book earns its disclosures by making them
precisely, not at length, and the full account is six hours away in the note.

## 7. Check: one narrator or two for Chapter 3?

Twenty-nine speaker lines. Proposed: **one narrator**, speaking the labels
("Kai." / "Me."). The alternative is two synthetic voices, which sounds more
like radio drama and makes the argument feel staged — whereas it was a real
argument conducted in text. One voice reading both sides is also, arguably, the
truer picture of what happened.

**My recommendation: one.** But you were there, and this is the chapter the
book's credibility rests on.

## 8. Check: does the Dictionary survive as audio?

Appendix A is kept in the script (~2,100 words, about fifteen minutes of
alphabetical definitions). It is short and in-voice, so I kept it — but
alphabetical reference material is the classic thing listeners skip, and
Appendix B ("The Standard Picture, in Brief") is prose and clearly stays.

**Keep, or cut Appendix A from the audio edition?** *Recommendation: keep.* It
costs a quarter hour and the terms it rescues are the ones a listener cannot
look up mid-walk.
